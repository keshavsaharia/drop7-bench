## The idea

Looking ahead in Drop7 is expensive because of luck: every disc dealt and
every hidden number revealed multiplies the tree, so a fair search stops after
a few moves. Determinized planning goes
around the tree. Write down one specific future: the next twenty discs in
order, and the number under every gray disc. With that future fixed there is
no luck left, and a deep search can solve the rest of the game well. Do this
for a handful of different invented futures, solve each one, and combine the
answers, usually by averaging each first move's value across the futures.
Each sampled future is a deterministic puzzle, hence the name.

## A small example

A two-day drive to a wedding. On day one you take the mountain road or the
coast road, three hours either way. On day two, from the mountain, you choose
at dawn between the pass and the detour, before you can see the sky. The pass
takes two hours if it is open and eight if it is closed, because you drive up,
find the gate shut and come back down. The detour takes five hours whatever
the weather. From the coast, day two is three and a half hours regardless. You
have three forecasts: two say the pass will be open, one says closed.

<DeterminizationThreeFutures />

A determinized planner solves each forecast as if it were certain. In the two
open forecasts it goes mountain, then pass: five hours. In the closed forecast
it goes mountain, then detour: eight hours. The average is six hours. The
coast is six and a half in every forecast, so the planner picks the mountain.

Now drive it. At dawn on day two you do not know which forecast came true. If
you pick the pass every time, day two averages four hours and the trip seven.
If you pick the detour every time, eight. The best a real driver can do on the
mountain is seven hours, and the coast is six and a half. The planner's six
hours credited you with one hour of knowledge you will not have, and that hour
flipped the decision. That is strategy fusion: three plans that cannot all be
followed by the same driver, fused into one number.

<DeterminizationStrategyFusion />

There are two honest ways out. Score one fixed plan across all three forecasts
(mountain then pass, seven; mountain then detour, eight; coast, six and a
half) and pick the coast. That is open-loop planning, and it cannot price
reacting to information you really will get, such as a sign at the foot of
the mountain. Or branch only on what you will be able to see, which is what a
fair search does.

## How it works

The [hindsight planner](/approaches/oracle-curriculum/hindsight-planner) and
the [open-loop lab](/approaches/heuristic-search/open-loop) share the recipe.

1. From the public position, invent K complete futures from a solver-local
   seed. The hindsight planner hashes the visible board and invents seven; the
   open-loop lab draws sixteen scenarios by default.
2. Give every candidate column the same futures. That is common random
   numbers: columns are compared on identical luck.
3. Inside each future, run a deep deterministic search. The hindsight planner
   searches to depth 8 with a beam of 64, treating the disc order and every
   hidden number as certain.
4. Combine the values per column, by the mean or by a blend of the mean and
   the lower quartile, so a column that is fatal in half the futures is not
   rewarded for its average.
5. Play the best column, throw the futures away, and invent fresh ones next
   move.

Where the futures come from decides whether the planner is legal. Invented
from a hash of the public board, it reads nothing a player cannot see. Copied
from the real hidden board and disc tape, it is an
[oracle](/learn/glossary), and its scores can only be diagnostics.

## In Drop7

The legal version lost by a wide margin. On its four-game
screen, under historical 7,000-point scoring, archival, ordinary depth-3
search averaged 107,076 points and 72.5 moves while the hindsight planner
averaged 51,500.5 points and 37.5 moves
([hindsight planner](/approaches/oracle-curriculum/hindsight-planner),
rejected, ledger-recorded). Four games is a screen; the confirmation seeds
were never opened. The ledger's diagnosis is the road-trip trap in Drop7
terms: "tape-specific later decisions make the
per-tape root values incompatible and overoptimistic".

Adding futures does not repair it. A later study on the
[flow ceiling](/approaches/lifetime-objective/flow-ceiling) swept the number
of futures for a clairvoyant planner, an oracle that reads the hidden board,
from 1 up to 1,024. Quality rose up to 256 futures and then fell: paired on
the same six tapes, mean lifetime was 182.00 moves with 256 futures and
100.33 with 1,024
([finding-12](/docs/exploratory/finding-12-fair-planner-ceiling-extended),
oracle, 6 of 8 games, scenario-engine scoring). Sampling noise hid the bias
at small counts; at large counts the planner converged on the wrong
objective.

The open-loop lab, which scores one fixed column sequence across its
scenarios, is support-only and has no retained result.
The [observable-state MCTS](/approaches/tree-search/observable-mcts) is the
other way out: it keys every tree node by the visible position alone, so no
later decision can depend on an unrevealed number.

## What it cannot do

It cannot price the ability to react. Strategy fusion overprices it, by
letting each plan react to a future it will not know; open-loop planning
underprices it, by forbidding reaction altogether. Neither error has been
measured on its own in Drop7.

More compute does not fix it: the 1,024-future arm above is the record's
clearest case of a bigger budget making the same estimator worse.
And the bias is largest on crowded boards, where the most is hidden and a
favourable reveal is the only thing that saves the game.

What survives is the pairing. Giving every column the same imagined futures
is reused across the [long-outcome experiments](/approaches/d4-long-outcome).
The open question is a determinized planner whose continuations are
restricted to public information at every step, which the
[lifetime objective](/approaches/lifetime-objective) family studies as the
fair planner: legal by construction and expensive by construction.
