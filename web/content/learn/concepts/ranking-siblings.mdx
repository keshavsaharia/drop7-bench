A look-ahead search ends at boards where the game is not over. It needs an
opinion about each one: *how promising is this?* That opinion is the **leaf
evaluator**, and almost every learning experiment in this repository is an
attempt to replace the hand-written one with something learned from data.

Most of those attempts failed in the same way. Any proposal to "just train a
bigger model" has to answer that failure.

## The job is to rank, not to predict

When a policy plays, it does not need to know *how many points* a board is
worth. It needs to know which of up to seven **sibling** moves is best. The
siblings are the columns available right now. A perfect ranking with wrong absolute
numbers plays perfectly. A model with tiny average error that gets the *order*
of two siblings wrong plays the wrong move.

## How the data was collected, and what it missed

The obvious way to get training data is to play games and write down what
happened: this board, this move, this outcome. Do that for thousands of games
and you have a large dataset. But look at what it contains for any one
position:

The model trains on the boards that were played and gets very good at
predicting their outcomes. Then it is deployed, shown seven siblings, and asked
to rank them. Six of the seven are boards whose futures were never measured.
Its answer for those is an **extrapolation** from boards that looked somewhat
similar, and if the behaviour policy had a consistent style (they all do), the
unplayed siblings are systematically the kinds of boards it never chose, so the
model is systematically worst exactly where it is being asked to decide.

The repository's status summary names this directly:

> A repeated problem was **sibling extrapolation**: a model learned the outcome
> of the action that was played, then deployment asked it to choose among
> several actions it had not observed equally well. Low value error on visited
> states did not guarantee good root-action ranking.

## How the failure was measured

An early diagnostic built a locked panel of 477 real positions and evaluated
**every** legal sibling at each one. The best learned ranker in that study
picked the same top move as fair D4 about 29% of the time, against D4's 38%
agreement with the long-outcome reference, and ordered sibling pairs correctly
about 60% of the time against D4's 67%. A conservative variant changed only 11
of 477 decisions, and those changes were not reliably better.

A regenerative policy-iteration variant overrode D4 on 11 roots; six overrides
helped, confidence bounds were negative, and five of eight origins did not

## What would fix it

The fix is conceptually simple and practically expensive: **label every
sibling**. For each training position, evaluate all legal columns under the
same random futures, then record every value. A dataset built that way is called
**successor-closed** here: every
successor the model will be asked about at play time is present in training.

That is precisely what the repository's registered afterstate theory proposes.
The proposal is direct: *train a single evaluator only on successor-closed
data, with every sibling labelled under aligned chance scenarios and a fixed
continuation policy, and it will rank root moves at least as well as fair D4 on
games it has never seen.*

**It was built and tested, and as tested it did not work.** Four pilot
experiments are on the record. The corpus pipeline labelled every legal sibling
under shared chance scenarios. The labels themselves turned out to be noisy:
with a handful of imagined
futures per sibling, re-running the same measurement on a different half of the
scenarios produced a substantially different ranking. Raising the number of
aligned futures to 256 fixed the stability problem, and the trained model still
did not rank moves better than fair D4. A final, more conservative experiment
let the model overrule the search only where the search's own top two moves were
nearly tied. That came closer than anything before it. It improved on fair D4
in both halves of the data, but the improvement in the second half was smaller
than the margin fixed in advance, so it is recorded as a **valid run with a
failed gate**, not as a success.

<TheorySummary id="TH-20260820-distributional-afterstate-ranker-7aba7fb3" />

The data shape was fixed, but that was not sufficient. The first stable
long-outcome model still had two disclosed limitations: a weak continuation
teacher and training stopped at 11 of 20 epochs. A later experiment removed
both ambiguities by giving a compact student successor-closed, exact D4 search
values for every legal sibling. It reached 0.375 top-1 agreement with D4,
below exact D1's 0.486. A smaller leaf-affordable student reached about 0.30.
The supported conclusion is narrower than "bigger models will work": sibling
coverage is necessary, but the tested compact board evaluators still could not
represent D4's ordering.

<Callout title="Played-move-only data fails at any scale" tone="warn">
Sibling labelling requires a search at every position, for every column, under
shared random scenarios. That work parallelises across a large cluster. Two
lessons carry forward. First,
compute spent generating *played-move-only* data reproduces the failure at any
scale; the data shape has to be right. Second, the pilot above shows that even
with the right shape, the number of imagined futures per label is itself a
compute dial that had to be turned up by 32× before the labels were stable
enough to learn from. The later exact-label failures show that stable labels
still do not guarantee a compact student can rank them. The concept page on
[whether more computation helps](/learn/concepts/does-more-compute-help)
picks both up.
</Callout>
