Drop7 gives you a disc and a decision: where should it go? A move can clear a
few discs straight away, or leave a useful arrangement for later. Learning to
see both is the start of playing ahead.

## The idea

A disc falls down the column you choose. It stops at the bottom or on another
disc. Then the game checks for matches.

<ChoiceLesson view="opening" />

A numbered disc clears when its number equals the number of touching discs
in its row or column, including itself. The other discs can have any number.
A gap ends the count. An isolated 1 clears; a 2 beside one other disc can clear.

Clears leave gaps. The discs above fall, which can create more matches.
Each new round of clears is a *wave*. Later waves in the same chain earn more
points per disc.

This is the part you control: the column. After the move settles, the game
deals another disc. That is the part you do not control: chance.

Column 6 is tempting. The 1 and 4 clear in the first wave for 14 points.
The 2 then falls beside the 3 and clears for another 39. Together, that is
53 points. It is the best move if we only count what happens now.

Column 1 earns just 7 points. The 1 clears on its own, leaving the rest of
the board as it was. That leaves something to work with on the next turn.

## How it works

### The disc you do not know yet

In the Hardcore version of Drop7 used here, the next disc can be any number
from 1 to 7. Each has the same chance: one in seven.

So imagine each possible disc in turn. For each one, find the reply that
earns the most points. You get to choose that reply after you see the disc.

The two boards below start *after* our first move. Select a possible next
disc to watch its best reply on each board. Start with the 2: the quieter
first move has left a much bigger chain available.

<ChoiceLesson view="chance" />

### Giving every possibility its share

A 2 would be helpful, but it might not arrive. To give all seven possibilities
the same weight, add their best reply scores and divide by seven. That is
the *average reply*.

Add the points from the first move to that average. The result is the move’s
*expected value* over these two moves. It describes an average across the
possible next discs; a single play will produce one of those outcomes.

Column 1 has an average reply worth about 69.1 points, giving it a two-move
value of about 76.1. Column 6 gives about 67.6. Looking one turn further
changes the choice.

<ChoiceLesson view="comparison" />

The buttons show what happens when we change the question. Counting only
today’s points favors column 6. Hoping for the luckiest disc favors column 4.
Preparing for the least helpful disc favors column 6. Averaging all the
possibilities favors columns 1, 2 and 3 equally.

This pattern has a name: *expectimax*. At a choice, take the best available
move. At a chance event, average the possible outcomes. The name can wait;
those two actions are the idea to keep.

## In Drop7

### Looking further ahead

We can repeat the same process for another turn. Choose a column, consider
what might be dealt, choose a reply, and continue. The number of moves we
look ahead is the search’s *depth*.

<ChoiceLesson view="depth" />

Each extra turn creates more possibilities to examine. Eventually the
search has to stop. It then needs an estimate of how useful the remaining
board is. That estimate is called a *board evaluator*.

The next lesson, [evaluating a board](/learn/concepts/evaluating-a-board),
looks at that question. You can also [try playing](/play) or see the
[full rules](/learn/rules), including gray discs and the rising floor.

<ChoiceLesson view="quiz" />

## What it cannot do

A better average does not promise a better result on every play. You can
make a sensible choice and still get an unhelpful disc.

This example compares the points from two moves on a small board. A full
game also asks you to make room for new discs and survive the rows that
rise from below. A move that earns more points soon can still leave a
difficult position later.

Gray discs <Disc solid size={22} /> add another kind of chance. Their numbers are hidden until
clears beside them open them. A player has to consider those possible
reveals too. This teaching board has no gray discs, so we can focus on
the next deal first.

The skill is to make a choice using what you can see, while leaving room
for what you cannot know yet.
