Score each column with six hand-made numbers about what the drop does right
now, learn the six weights from experience, and find out how much of Drop7
that can see. That is Erez Klein and Ben Friedmann's Stanford CS221 final
project, "Drop7", the only outside attempt at this game the repository knows
of. This page reproduces it from its own code, ports it onto the repository's
rules engine, and measures where it stops.

<EvidenceLabel status="completed" evidence="reproduced" reads="public" />

Everything the policy reads is public: the visible board, the visible next
disc, and (unused by the six features) the moves until the next rise. The
learner sees a reward while training and nothing else.

## The intuition

Imagine a player who only asks one question per column: *what happens the
instant my disc lands there?* Does it clear? Does it make its row or its
column clear? Does the clear land next to gray discs and crack them? Is this
the lowest column, so the board stays flat? Six such questions, each answered
with a small count, each multiplied by a learned weight, and the column with
the biggest total wins. Nothing about the position two moves from now, nothing
about which numbers are buried where, nothing about the [rise clock](/learn/glossary).

The report's six features, as the code computes them (the paper's prose
differs in two places, noted in the technical record):

1. **Lowest column**: 1 if the disc would land at the board's minimum height.
2. **Row detonations**: how many discs already in the landing row would clear
   once the disc joins their run, each counted 1 plus the number of gray
   neighbours it touches (a cracked neighbour counts double).
3. **Column detonations**: the same count for discs below the landing cell,
   plus the landing cell's own gray-neighbour count whether or not anything
   clears.
4. **Tallest column**: the column-detonation count again, but only when the
   disc lands at the maximum height and at most two columns share it.
5. **A clearing 1**: 1 plus gray adjacency when the disc is a 1 that clears.
6. **The disc clears**: 1 plus gray adjacency when the dropped disc clears by
   its row run or its column height.

The learning rule is textbook [Q-learning](/learn/glossary) with linear
function approximation: after each drop, nudge the six weights so that the
column's score moves toward "reward now plus the best score available next
turn". The reward is one point per move survived. The report then tests the
frozen weights with no exploration.

## How it works, step by step

1. **Read the position.** Visible board and visible next disc.
2. **For every legal column, compute the six features** of dropping there.
3. **Score each column** as the weighted sum. Ties go to the rightmost column
   (a quirk of Python's tuple comparison in the original, kept deliberately).
4. **Play the best column.** In the original simulator the agent may also pick
   a full column and lose on the spot; the port restricts both the greedy
   choice and exploration to legal columns.
5. **While training, update the weights** from the observed reward and the
   best successor score, with a step size of one over the number of moves
   played so far, exploration probability of one over the fourth root of that
   count, and a ridge penalty of 0.1.

## What happened

**The report reproduces from its own code.** Three independent training runs
of 50,000 games in the authors' simulator test at 49.08, 49.05 and 49.02 moves
over 10,000 games each; the report says 49.61. Uniform-random play scores
31.66, 31.73 and 31.85; the report says 31.2. Both preregistered bands pass
([RS-20260902T082726Z-75606ce7](/research/results/RS-20260902T082726Z-75606ce7)).

**Almost all of that training is idle.** Because the step size is one over the
number of *moves*, not games, the weights are effectively frozen after a few
hundred games. A learner stopped after 300 games tests at 49.08 moves on the
same 10,000 games as the 50,000-game learner, also 49.08. The report's
explanation of its own stability, the ridge penalty, does not hold for the
shipped configuration either: with the penalty removed the learner tests at
48.97, not below random as the report's Figure 5 states. The step size has
already shrunk to a hundred-thousandth by the time the penalty could matter.

**On the repository's corrected Hardcore rules the six features are a
survival heuristic, and a real one.** Re-learned with the same schedule in the
Rust port, the policy lives 35.8 moves and scores 107,147 points on average
over 256 previously evaluated development games, against 26.25 moves and
73,263 points for uniform-random legal play on the same games: a paired gain
of 9.5 moves with a one-sided 95% lower bound of 8.85. That is real but small
next to the reference: fair depth-4 search averaged 308,296 points on its
64-game development cohort, and on the first 32 of these same seeds the
depth-4 search with seven chance samples scored 380,205 points and lived 110.4 moves, beating every six-feature arm on at least 30 of the 32 games.

**The learned weights are an accident of the first hundred moves.** The
weights the authors' own simulator learns, transplanted unchanged onto the
Rust engine, live 40.5 moves and score 123,968 points, beating the weights
learned *in* the Rust engine by 4.7 moves (lower bound 3.8). Same features,
same algorithm, different opening experience: the authors' board starts empty,
the real game starts with a gray row, and a step size that dies within a few
hundred moves locks in whatever it saw first. Neither set of weights is the
best six-weight policy; the temporal-difference fixed point is simply not it.

**A direct search over the six weights beat both learned vectors in five
seconds.** Since the policy is just an argmax over six weights, the weights
can be searched directly against whole-game lifetime instead of learned by
temporal difference. A cross-entropy search (population 64, 30 generations,
256 paired training games per generation) converged by generation ten; its
frozen optimum, re-selected on a fresh 1,024-game block and then read once on
the pilot cohort, lives 44.8 moves and scores 138,973 points: 4.3 moves over
the transplanted weights (lower bound 2.8) and 18.6 over random (lower bound
17.2). The optimum puts its largest weight on "a 1 that clears" and almost
none on the tallest-column feature; its direction is only 0.55 cosine from the
engine-learned vector. So the temporal-difference plateau was the optimizer's,
not the features'. The features' own ceiling, at least for this search, is
about 45 moves and 140,000 points
([RS-20260902T084356Z-784ebf14](/research/results/RS-20260902T084356Z-784ebf14)).

**Slower step sizes did not raise the ceiling; two of them diverged.** A
constant step of 0.001 gained 0.5 moves over the original schedule (lower
bound 0.12), the same size as the gap between two identical-configuration
seeds (0.48). A per-game step and a slowly decaying step both blew the weights
up, to a billion and to NaN, which is the deadly-triad instability the report
described and which its own 1/t schedule had hidden. Rewarding corrected score
instead of survival changed nothing measurable.

## What this taught us, and what is still open

**Reproducibility is fine; the explanation was not.** The numbers reproduce
to within a move, but the report's account of *why* the method worked, ridge
regularisation taming divergence, is not what the shipped code does. The code
is stable because its step size collapses, and it learns for the same reason
only in its first few hundred games. Any future use of this report should
cite its numbers and not its mechanism.

**Six first-wave features are a survival prior worth about ten moves.** Under
Hardcore scoring that is roughly 35,000 points over random, an order of
magnitude short of the depth-4 reference. This agrees with the repository's
recurring finding that lifetime is the learnable quantity and chain structure
is not, and it puts a number on how much of lifetime purely local features
capture.

**When the policy is six numbers, search them; do not learn them.** Two
temporal-difference weight vectors differed by 4.7 moves and a five-second
direct search beat both. The general lesson for this repository is the one it
keeps relearning from the other side: a value-prediction objective is not the
control objective, and for tiny policy families whole-game search is cheaper
and better. The ceiling of these six features is about 45 moves, an order of
magnitude below depth-4 in points, so no SCREEN against depth-4 on fresh seeds
is warranted by any arm here. What remains open is whether "a 1 that clears
next to gray" or "lands on a lowest column" earns a place as a cheap correction
term inside the depth-4 leaf, which is a different experiment.
