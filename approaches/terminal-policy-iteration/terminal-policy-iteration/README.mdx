The full-strength version of the family's idea: 255 shared imagined futures,
200 moves each, and a rule that only changes the reference's move when it is
99% sure of a gain in both score and lifetime.

<EvidenceLabel status="rejected" evidence="task-record only" reads="public" />

## The intuition

Its cheaper sibling,
[public rollout policy iteration](/approaches/terminal-policy-iteration/public-rollout-policy-iteration),
compared columns with fifteen imagined futures over fifty moves and could not
tell the good ones apart reliably enough to act. The obvious diagnosis is
sampling noise, and the obvious remedy is more samples and a longer look: 255
futures instead of 15, 200 moves instead of 50 — long enough for forty row
rises, which is the timescale on which a policy actually dies.

The second change is stricter arithmetic. Instead of taking whichever column
has the best average, this program demands that the paired difference against
the reference's own move clear a one-sided **99%** lower confidence bound — and
it demands that separately for corrected score *and* for moves survived. A
column that scores more but lives less does not qualify. Neither does a column
that is merely probably better.

## How it works, step by step

1. Read the public position only: board, visible next disc, moves until the
   next rise, terminal flag.
2. Build 255 event-stratified chance streams and give **every sibling column
   the same 255**, so all columns face identical luck.
3. For each column, force that move; every later decision is made by a fresh,
   completed fair one-move-ahead search whose input type can hold nothing but
   public information.
4. Run each continuation out to a 200-move horizon and record score and moves
   survived.
5. Replace the reference's action only when the paired one-sided 99% lower
   bounds are positive on *both* measures. Otherwise keep it.

## What happened

It found something, and the something was not big enough.

The retained verdict is that the gain was real but missed the frozen
score-and-survival gate and the requirement that improvement hold up across
whole origin games rather than in aggregate. That combination: a genuine
effect that will not survive being split by game — is the single most common
outcome in this repository, and it is what the whole-origin condition exists to
detect: a mean can be lifted by one lucky origin while most origins get worse.

There is nothing to quote. This
result is **task-record only**: it comes from a research conversation and was
never promoted into the written ledger, so there is no protocol, no artifact
hash, no per-game data and no number.
[Audit 04](/docs/exploratory/audit-04-blind-spots) lists this source among the
thirty rejections that "cannot be re-derived", and specifically flags it as one
of the survival-flavoured experiments whose numbers a later analysis needed and
could not find. Treat "the gain was real" as an unverified claim.

## What this taught us, and what is still open

- **More samples did not rescue the comparison.** Going from 15 futures to 255
  and from 50 moves to 200 produced an effect that still could not pass a
  per-origin check. That is evidence about the size of the available
  improvement, not just about the noise.
- **What is missing is provenance.** A rerun of this exact program with a
  registered protocol and retained per-game output would convert the most
  interesting claim in this family from an anecdote into evidence, without
  needing a new idea.
- **What was ruled out** is this configuration at this budget. What was not
  ruled out is the objective: judging moves by measured lifetime remains the
  motivation for the whole family.
